# 1. Joint vertex-edge coordinates

The mark-only analysis gives the complete support-order spectrum for
statistics generated by independent vertex variables. A stochastic ordered DAG
contains a second independent family: one pair variable for each unordered
vertex pair. The full graph statistic is therefore not an ordinary
vertex-sampled $U$-statistic, but a generalized $U$-statistic on a dissociated
vertex-edge array.

Let

```math
(X_i)_{i\geq1}
```

be independent variables with common law $\mu$, and let

```math
(Y_{ij})_{1\leq i<j<\infty}
```

be independent variables with common law $\nu$, independent of the vertex
variables. The variable $X_i$ carries data attached to vertex $i$, while
$Y_{ij}$ carries irreducible pair data attached to $\{i,j\}$.

Fix $k\geq1$, and let

```math
f=f\left((X_i)_{i\in[k]},(Y_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\right)
```

be square-integrable and invariant under simultaneous permutations of the $k$
vertex labels. Define

```math
\theta_f=\mathbb E[f]
```

and the induced generalized $U$-statistic

```math
T_n(f)
=
\frac1{(n)_k}
\sum_{\phi:[k]\hookrightarrow[n]}
f_\phi,
```

where $(n)_k=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$, the sum is over injections, and $f_\phi$
is obtained by replacing every local vertex $i$ by $\phi(i)$. Since $f$ is
symmetric, this is also the average over the $k$-element vertex subsets of
$[n]$.

Every finite induced-DAG density in an ordered-kernel sector has this form.
The vertex variables encode the latent marks, and the pair variables encode
the independent uniforms or Bernoulli residuals used to generate edges.

# 2. Looped support patterns

The primitive-coordinate subsets of $f$ admit a graph representation. A
selected vertex variable $X_i$ is represented by a loop at $i$, and a selected
pair variable $Y_{ij}$ is represented by an ordinary edge $ij$.

A support pattern $P$ is therefore a finite simple graph in which loops are
allowed, with no isolated vertices. Its primitive coordinate set is

```math
\mathcal C(P)
=
\{X_i:ii\in E(P)\}
\cup
\{Y_{ij}:i<j,\ ij\in E(P)\}.
```

Write

```math
v(P)=|V(P)|,
\qquad
c(P)=|\mathcal C(P)|.
```

The first number counts supporting vertices, while the second counts primitive
random coordinates.

For a labeled support pattern $P$ contained in the looped complete graph on
$[k]$, let $\mathscr F_P$ be the sigma-field generated by $\mathcal C(P)$.
Define the primitive-coordinate projection

```math
\Pi_Pf
=
\sum_{Q\subseteq P}
(-1)^{c(P)-c(Q)}
\mathbb E[f\mid\mathscr F_Q].
```

Here $Q\subseteq P$ means deletion of any collection of loops and ordinary
edges; vertices that become isolated carry no coordinate and may be suppressed.

## Proposition 1. Canonicality and orthogonality

For every proper subpattern $Q\subsetneq P$,

```math
\mathbb E[\Pi_Pf\mid\mathscr F_Q]=0.
```

Moreover,

```math
f-\theta_f
=
\sum_{\varnothing\neq P\subseteq K_k^\circ}
\Pi_Pf,
```

where $K_k^\circ$ is the complete graph on $[k]$ with one loop at each vertex,
and projections belonging to distinct labeled primitive-coordinate subsets are
orthogonal in $L^2$.

## Proof

The primitive coordinates are mutually independent. Möbius inversion on their
Boolean lattice gives the pointwise decomposition. Conditioning a projection
on a proper coordinate subset pairs every term containing a deleted coordinate
with the corresponding term omitting it, and the two contributions cancel.

For distinct coordinate subsets $P$ and $Q$, choose a primitive coordinate
belonging to one but not the other. The corresponding projection has
conditional expectation zero when that coordinate is integrated, while the
other projection is independent of it. Their inner product is therefore zero.

The decomposition is finer than the mark-only Hoeffding decomposition. It
distinguishes, for example, a two-vertex mark interaction, a centered pair
variable, and an interaction containing both marks and the pair variable, even
though all three components have the same vertex support.

# 3. Exact support-pattern decomposition

Let $\mathfrak P_k$ contain one representative of every nonempty looped
support-pattern isomorphism class with at most $k$ vertices. For
$P\in\mathfrak P_k$, choose a labeled embedding of $P$ into $[k]$, set

```math
f_P=\Pi_Pf,
```

and write $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ for the automorphism group preserving both
loops and ordinary edges. Symmetry of $f$ implies that $f_P$ is invariant
under $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.

For $r=v(P)$, define

```math
U_{n,P}(f_P)
=
\frac1{(n)_r}
\sum_{\psi:V(P)\hookrightarrow[n]}
(f_P)_\psi.
```

## Theorem 2. Exact joint vertex-edge decomposition

For every $n\geq k$,

```math
T_n(f)-\theta_f
=
\sum_{P\in\mathfrak P_k}
\frac{(k)_{v(P)}}{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|}
U_{n,P}(f_P).
```

## Proof

Start with the labeled primitive-coordinate decomposition of Proposition 1 and
average it over all injections $\phi:[k]\hookrightarrow[n]$. Suppose that a
labeled coordinate subset has support $R\subseteq[k]$ of size $r$. For every
fixed injection $\psi:R\hookrightarrow[n]$, there are exactly

```math
(n-r)_{k-r}
```

extensions to an injection of $[k]$. Hence its averaged contribution is

```math
\frac{(n-r)_{k-r}}{(n)_k}
\sum_{\psi:R\hookrightarrow[n]}(f_P)_\psi
=
U_{n,P}(f_P).
```

The number of labeled coordinate subsets in $K_k^\circ$ isomorphic to $P$ is

```math
\frac{(k)_r}{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|}.
```

Grouping the equal contributions by isomorphism class gives the formula.

This identity is exact at every finite level. It does not use a continuum
approximation or an asymptotic expansion.

# 4. Exact nested covariance

The covariance of the support-pattern decomposition is diagonal in the
isomorphism class of the pattern. Let $f$ and $g$ be symmetric kernels on $k$
and $\ell$ vertices, respectively. For any support pattern $P$ embeddable in
both kernels, let $f_P$ and $g_P$ denote their corresponding canonical
projections.

## Theorem 3. Support-pattern covariance identity

For $n\leq m$,

```math
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
U_{n,P}(f_P),
U_{m,Q}(g_Q)
\right)
=0
```

unless $P$ and $Q$ are isomorphic. For a common representative $P$ with
$r=v(P)$,

```math
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
U_{n,P}(f_P),
U_{m,P}(g_P)
\right)
=
\frac{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|}{(m)_r}
\mathbb E[f_Pg_P].
```

Consequently,

```math
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
T_n(f),T_m(g)
\right)
=
\sum_P
\frac{(k)_{v(P)}(\ell)_{v(P)}}
{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|(m)_{v(P)}}
\mathbb E[f_Pg_P].
```

## Proof

Expand both support averages over injections. If two embedded
primitive-coordinate sets differ, one of them contains a coordinate absent
from the other. Integrating that coordinate annihilates the corresponding
canonical projection, so the covariance vanishes.

Two embedded copies of the same representative $P$ have identical
primitive-coordinate sets exactly when their injections differ by an
automorphism of $P$. There are

```math
(n)_r|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|
```

such matching ordered pairs with the first copy contained in $[n]$. Each
contributes $\mathbb E[f_Pg_P]$. Division by $(n)_r(m)_r$ gives the second
formula. Substitution into Theorem 2 gives the full covariance identity.

For $f=g$ and $n=m$, this becomes

```math
\operatorname{Var}(T_n(f))
=
\sum_P
\frac{(k)_{v(P)}^2}
{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|(n)_{v(P)}}
\|f_P\|_2^2.
```

The formula provides the complete stable second-order coefficient hierarchy of
every finite graph observable in a dissociated vertex-edge sector.

# 5. Vertex-support degree and logarithmic spectrum

For each $r$, define the order-$r$ component

```math
T_n^{[r]}(f)
=
\sum_{\substack{P\in\mathfrak P_k\\v(P)=r}}
\frac{(k)_r}{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|}
U_{n,P}(f_P).
```

Its stable cross coefficient with a kernel $g$ is

```math
C_r(f,g)
=
\sum_{\substack{P\\v(P)=r}}
\frac{(k)_r(\ell)_r}{|\operatorname{Aut}(P)|}
\mathbb E[f_Pg_P].
```

The exact covariance identity gives, for $n\leq m$,

```math
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
T_n^{[r]}(f),T_m^{[r]}(g)
\right)
=
\frac{C_r(f,g)}{(m)_r}.
```

Let

```math
m_N(s)=\lfloor Ne^s\rfloor.
```

Then

```math
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
 m_N(s)^{r/2}T_{m_N(s)}^{[r]}(f),
 m_N(t)^{r/2}T_{m_N(t)}^{[r]}(g)
\right)\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow
C_r(f,g)e^{-r|s-t|/2}.
\end{aligned}
```

Thus the logarithmic relaxation rate is determined by the number of supporting
vertices:

```math
\boxed{
\text{vertex support }r
\Longrightarrow
n^{r/2}\text{ normalization}
\Longrightarrow
\text{covariance decay rate }\frac r2
}
```

for the complete joint vertex-edge hierarchy.

The number $c(P)$ of primitive coordinates does not determine the rate. A
single centered edge variable has one primitive coordinate but two supporting
vertices, so it has $n$-scale fluctuations and rate $1$. A product of two
centered vertex marks also has two primitive coordinates and two supporting
vertices, and therefore has the same rate despite a different limiting law.

Define the principal vertex-support degree by

```math
d(f)
=
\min
\left
\{
v(P):\|f_P\|_2>0
\right\}.
```

Then

```math
n^{d(f)}\operatorname{Var}(T_n(f))
\longrightarrow
C_{d(f)}(f,f).
```

Every higher support order is negligible under the normalization $n^{d(f)/2}$.

# 6. Topology and limiting law

Vertex support determines the normalization and covariance decay, while the
topology of a principal support pattern controls whether the limit is Gaussian
or composite.

A pattern consisting of two isolated loops is disconnected. Its normalized
average is a second-order empirical-mark chaos and generally has a centered
quadratic-Gaussian limit. A pattern containing one ordinary edge is connected.
Its average combines $O(n^2)$ pair variables and generally has a Gaussian
limit.

The generalized $U$-statistic theory of Janson and Nowicki indexes canonical
projections by support graphs of vertex and pair variables. In that theory,
connected principal support graphs give Gaussian fixed-time limits, while
disconnected principal support graphs permit non-Gaussian polynomial or chaos
limits. The looped support patterns above are the direct one-type version of
that construction: loops represent the vertex variables and ordinary edges
represent the pair variables.

The distinction is therefore

```math
\text{support size}
\quad\text{controls scale and decay,}
```

while

```math
\text{support topology}
\quad\text{controls Gaussian versus composite behavior.}
```

Several nonisomorphic principal patterns may occur at the same support order.
Their orthogonal contributions then share one logarithmic rate but can have
different distributional types.

# 7. Symmetric two-layer edge density

The stochastic two-layer edge density gives the smallest complete example. At
equal layer weights, encode the layer of vertex $i$ by an independent
Rademacher variable

```math
S_i\in\{-1,1\},
```

and write

```math
\xi_{ij}=B_{ij}-r,
```

where $B_{ij}$ is Bernoulli with parameter $r$. The edge indicator on
$\{i,j\}$ is

```math
E_{ij}
=
\frac{1-S_iS_j}{2}
(r+\xi_{ij}).
```

Its centered primitive-coordinate decomposition is exactly

```math
E_{ij}-\frac r2
=
-\frac r2S_iS_j
+
\frac12\xi_{ij}
-
\frac12S_iS_j\xi_{ij}.
```

The three terms correspond to three support patterns on two vertices:

1. two loops and no ordinary edge;
2. one ordinary edge and no loops;
3. one ordinary edge with loops at both endpoints.

All three have vertex support two and therefore rate $1$. The first is
disconnected and the other two are connected.

Their stable variance contributions are

```math
\frac{r^2}{2},
\qquad
\frac{r(1-r)}{2},
\qquad
\frac{r(1-r)}{2},
```

respectively. The total order-two coefficient is therefore

```math
\frac{r^2}{2}+r(1-r).
```

The disconnected component gives the quadratic term

```math
r\left(\frac12-2Y^2\right),
```

while the two connected components combine into the Gaussian rate-one process
with stationary variance

```math
r(1-r).
```

This recovers the mixed limit derived previously and explains its structure
without an observable-specific Taylor expansion. The non-Gaussian and Gaussian
terms coexist because they belong to different support topologies at the same
vertex-support order.

# 8. Two-step routes

At equal weights in the stochastic three-layer sector, the two-step route
density has the same structural split. Its order-two mark projection is
supported on two disconnected loops and gives the quadratic term

```math
\frac{2ab}{3}-ab\|Y\|^2.
```

Its irreducible $12$- and $23$-edge residuals are supported on connected
two-vertex patterns and give the Gaussian processes

```math
bZ_{12}+aZ_{23}.
```

The common support size explains the shared rate $1$. The different support
topology explains why one part belongs to a second Gaussian chaos while the
other is Gaussian.

The path-sensitive result is therefore a special case of the complete joint
support-pattern decomposition rather than an isolated cancellation.

# 9. Stable coefficients for all finite DAG patterns

Let $F$ be any finite induced-DAG pattern in a stochastic ordered-kernel
sector. Express its indicator kernel as a function of the finite vertex marks
and pair uniforms. Theorem 2 produces a finite family of canonical looped
support patterns $P$, and Theorem 3 gives every variance and cross-covariance
coefficient by the finite integrals

```math
\mathbb E[f_Pg_P].
```

In a finite-layer sector these are finite sums and elementary one-dimensional
integrals. Once the layer partition and block probabilities are recovered from
the endpoint graph, all coefficients are graph-intrinsically estimable.

The stable-coefficient problem is therefore solved for the complete
finite-pattern algebra of every recovered finite ordered-kernel sector. The
remaining issue is distributional rather than second-order: determine the
joint functional limits generated by arbitrary collections of connected and
disconnected principal support patterns.

For a connected support pattern $P$ on $r$ vertices, the expected sequential
limit is a Gaussian process of the form

```math
\sigma_P e^{-rs/2}B(e^{rs}),
```

which is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with relaxation rate $r/2$.
Disconnected patterns should produce products and contractions of the
connected Gaussian fields.

# 10. Literature relation

S. Janson and K. Nowicki, *The asymptotic distributions of generalized
U-statistics with applications to random graphs*, Probability Theory and
Related Fields 90 (1991), 341-375, introduced generalized $U$-statistics with
independent vertex and pair variables and used support graphs to classify their
asymptotic distributions in random-graph applications. Their principal support
order determines normalization, and connected principal support graphs yield
Gaussian limits.

Recent work on network $U$-statistics has formulated analogous orthogonal
decompositions directly in terms of subgraphs of Aldous-Hoover-Kallenberg
variables. The looped support formalism used here specializes that construction
to a one-type dissociated array and makes the logarithmic-size covariance
spectrum explicit.

The additional result needed by the present relational-growth program is the
exact nested-level identity of Theorem 3. It converts support size into the
stationary logarithmic covariance

```math
 e^{-r|s-t|/2}
```

and identifies the stable coefficient of every finite DAG observable before any
limiting-law theorem is invoked.
